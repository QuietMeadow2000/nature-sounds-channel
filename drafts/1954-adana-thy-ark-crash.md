## SCRIPT

At 1:40 in the afternoon on April 3rd, 1954, a Turkish Airlines aircraft named "ARK" lifted off from Adana, bound for the capital, Ankara. It carried passengers and crew on what should have been a routine domestic flight.

Ten minutes later, near Kurttepe, at the foot of the Taurus Mountains, the aircraft broke apart in mid-air.

Wreckage scattered across a fifteen-kilometer stretch of terrain. Twenty-five people were killed. Among them was Remzi Oğuz Arık — a respected archaeologist and statesman. Also aboard was Güler Karaduman, Turkey's first female flight attendant.

The exact cause was never definitively established. What's known is that the explosion occurred roughly ten minutes after takeoff — a timing that has long suggested a technical failure, though no confirmed conclusion was ever reached.

It became the first major civil aviation disaster in Turkish history, and it stayed in the public consciousness for a long time afterward — not just for the lives lost, but for who was lost.

Twenty years later, Turkish Airlines would be involved in a far larger tragedy: the 1974 crash of a DC-10 near Ermenonville, north of Paris, when a cargo door failure brought down a flight carrying 346 people — to this day one of the deadliest accidents in aviation history.

But it began, two decades earlier, with a single aircraft over the Taurus foothills, and twenty-five names that a nation did not forget.
